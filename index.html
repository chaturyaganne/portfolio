01 / The opportunity

Buying tickets is one decision.
Planning together is several.

For a group outing, the organizer is making a decision on behalf of people with different budgets, preferences, and access needs. A promising design opportunity is to put those constraints next to the tickets, so the organizer can reason about the whole night rather than just the lowest price.

How might we help a group choose tickets with confidence, without making one person hold every constraint in their head?
Evidence boundary. This is a self-initiated concept inspired by the supplied SeatGeek UX New Grad brief. It is not commissioned by, affiliated with, or a redesign shipped at SeatGeek. No participants were interviewed. Personas, pain points, and expected benefits below are hypotheses to test.

Start from what already exists

SeatGeek already displays all-in prices, and its help documentation describes an Accessible filter. Those are useful foundations, not missing features I can claim to have discovered. Encore combines these concepts with persistent group constraints and side-by-side trade-offs in a fictional ticketing flow.

02 / Understanding the task

Design around the organizer.
Keep every fan in the picture.

Working proto-persona: an organizer choosing four seats for friends. This role is a design aid, not a research finding. The prototype task is to choose four adjacent seats at no more than $100 per person, with a step-free route and a clear view.

Hypothesis 01

“What will we each pay?”

Showing per-person and group totals together may reduce calculation and uncertainty.

Hypothesis 02

“Does this work for everyone?”

Keeping seating needs visible may prevent selections that miss a group requirement.

Hypothesis 03

“Which trade-off is worth it?”

A comparison of price, view, and route information may make compromises easier to discuss.

A small journey with explicit decision points

1. Set group needsParty size, all-in budget, step-free route, clear view.
2. Explore optionsBrowse fictional events. Retain constraints when opening seats.
3. Compare seatsSee price, route and view facts side by side.
4. Review the planCheck the exact group total and copy a plan to share.

The prototype has seeded inventory rather than a live marketplace. Adjacent-seat availability is an explicit property of each sample listing. Access information is illustrative; a real product would need authoritative venue data, refresh rules, and a clear way to resolve uncertainty.

03 / Design decisions

Three decisions that shape
the experience.

01

Make the budget a group rule, not a mental note.

The budget is all-in and per person. The party size and total stay visible when browsing seats. Changing either recalculates results and the review. This is an exact arithmetic behavior, not a promise about a real ticket marketplace.

02

Explain fit instead of inventing a “best deal” score.

Every listing describes the view and access route in text. A map provides orientation, but the same section can be selected from a labeled list. I chose observable attributes over an opaque score that this dataset cannot justify.

03

Let people compare, then carry the decision forward.

Compare up to three listings on the same criteria. Selecting one opens a review with base price, fees, per-person total and group total. Copying the plan does not send a message or create a live shared session. The recipient gets plain text they can discuss with the organizer.

No countdown. No fake scarcity. No payment collection.The final action ends a prototype task, not a purchase.

05 / Validation

Working interactions.
Research still to do.

The implementation includes automated behavior checks for the core journey, pricing, filtering, comparison, and recovery states. Those checks establish software behavior. They do not measure whether fans find the design intuitive, accessible, or useful.

Research status: no participant study has been run. There is no claimed conversion lift, task-success rate, satisfaction score, or usability improvement. A prototype session can record its own elapsed time and actions locally; that is an individual session record, not a study result.

A test plan ready to run

  1. Recruit five adult participants with varied event-buying experience; include participants with access needs through consent-based recruiting. Five is a proposed first round, not a sample already tested.
  2. Ask each participant to choose four adjacent seats under $100 per person, with a step-free route and a clear view. Observe behavior before offering guidance.
  3. Ask them to compare two options, explain the total, and recover from an impossible budget. Probe which information they trust and what remains unclear.
  4. Record task completion, critical errors, price comprehension, first-click choices and a post-task ease rating. Treat results as directional qualitative evidence; do not generalize a tiny convenience sample.
  5. Review findings with a designer, PM and engineer if collaborators are available. Prioritize observed issues, revise, and repeat. Those collaborative sessions have not happened yet.

Accessibility choices and limits

Visible labels, text-based seat attributes, keyboard controls, a comparison dialog with focus handling, reduced-motion styles and responsive layouts are implemented. These decisions follow WAI labeling guidance and visible-focus guidance. They are not a WCAG certification or a substitute for manual audits and assistive-technology testing.

06 / Handoff & reflection

A small system.
A deliberate point of view.

The visual system balances an editorial event-poster feel with quiet utility: warm paper, dark green, a lime accent, generous typography and simple bordered controls. Color never carries the only meaning of a ticket attribute.

What I would do next

Validate whether group constraints deserve this much prominence. Test how people distinguish a step-free route from accessible seating, and how they handle incomplete venue information. If those assumptions do not hold, simplify the flow rather than adding features. A real launch would also require venue-supplied inventory, permission-aware shared planning, pricing/tax rules, payment processing, security review and product analytics.
